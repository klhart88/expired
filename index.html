Step 01

Why didn't the home sell?

Usually there is no single explanation. The most useful review is factual, respectful, and focused on what buyers actually saw and did.

01
Price versus competing sales

Buyers compare your asking price with recently sold homes and available alternatives. Even a well-kept property can struggle when its value proposition is unclear.

02
Condition and presentation

Repairs, staging, photography, lighting, and first impressions can influence whether buyers schedule a visit or keep scrolling. Not every improvement is worth its cost.

03
Marketing and buyer reach

Listing visibility, descriptions, imagery, distribution, and showing access all matter. Exposure should be evaluated alongside actual engagement—not assumed from the listing alone.

04
Timing and market conditions

Inventory, financing costs, seasonality, and buyer demand can shift during a listing period. The strategy should reflect the market you are entering now, not only the one you entered before.

Review the strategy, not the people.

The goal is not to assign blame to a previous agent. It is to identify what the available evidence suggests—and what, if anything, should change before your next decision.

Step 02

What the data can tell you about your home

County-wide numbers make headlines, but buyers compare your home with the handful of homes they could buy instead. These five measures tell the real story.

01
Sold versus expired, nearby.

In your price range and area, how many similar homes sold, and how many expired?

02
Days on market for homes that sold.

If they sold in 20 days and yours sat for 120, the gap is a clue.

03
Sale price versus list price.

Did nearby homes sell at, above or below asking, and after how many price changes?

04
Today’s competition.

How many similar homes are for sale right now, and at what prices?

05
Showing and feedback activity.

Were buyers coming but not offering, or not coming at all? Those point to different fixes.

Example (illustrative, not real data)

A 3-bedroom home listed at $339,900 stayed on the market 142 days. Over the same period, eight similar homes within a mile sold, after a median of 24 days, at about $325,000. That pattern points first to price positioning, not marketing.

A sold-versus-expired comparison is a snapshot of listing outcomes—it is not a prediction that any particular home will sell or expire.

Step 03

You have more than one next move.

Each path can be reasonable depending on your goals, carrying costs, timing, and the property's condition. The trade-offs deserve a clear look.

01
Relist with a revised plan

Potential upside: Reintroduce the home with improved pricing, presentation, or reach. Trade-off: Additional preparation, carrying costs, and no guarantee of an offer.

02
Rent the property

Potential upside: Keep ownership while generating possible rental income. Trade-off: Landlord responsibilities, vacancy, maintenance, financing, insurance, and local rules.

03
Wait and reassess

Potential upside: More time to prepare, address repairs, or reconsider your move. Trade-off: Ongoing ownership expenses and an uncertain future market.

04
Explore an as-is sale

Potential upside: Reduce some repair or preparation work. Trade-off: Buyers may price in repair costs and risk; proceeds and terms can differ substantially from a traditional listing.

Step 04

Five questions worth asking before you decide.

Use this quick self-check to identify what deserves closer attention. It is a planning tool, not a diagnosis of why the listing expired.

    Before you relist

    What not to assume.

    A fresh listing can be valuable, but a new start is most useful when it is supported by a thoughtful change in strategy.

    Pricing
    A price reduction alone may not solve everything.

    Consider whether the price, condition, marketing, and competing inventory are working together.

    Marketing
    More views do not automatically mean more offers.

    Review the quality of inquiries, showing activity, buyer feedback, and how the property compares.

    Timing
    Waiting does not guarantee a stronger market.

    Compare the cost of holding the property with your flexibility and next-home plans.

    Helpful clarity

    Common questions after a listing expires.

    Understand the basics before you make another commitment.

    Does an expired listing mean my home is worth less?Not necessarily. A listing outcome and market value are not the same thing.
    An expired listing means the listing agreement reached its expiration without a completed sale through that listing. A current CMA should consider comparable sales, condition, competition, and market changes.
    Can I immediately relist with someone else?Check your prior agreement and any continuing obligations first.
    Review the expiration date, any protection or holdover period, and other contract terms. If anything is unclear, consult the prior brokerage or a qualified real estate attorney before signing a new agreement.
    Should I keep the same asking price?Only after reviewing the evidence and current competition.
    A fresh market analysis can help distinguish a pricing issue from presentation, access, exposure, or timing factors. Keeping or changing the price should be a reasoned decision.
    Will a new listing guarantee a sale?No strategy can guarantee a buyer, price, or closing date.
    The purpose of a revised plan is to make better-informed decisions and improve how the property is positioned—not to promise a particular outcome.
